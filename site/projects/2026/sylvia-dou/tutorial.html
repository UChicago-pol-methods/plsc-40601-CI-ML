Large-scale text data are increasingly labeled by faster tool such as LLMs nowadays. We then use those labels as the outcome in a causal analysis. Let YiY_i be the true (gold) outcome, Ỹi\widetilde{Y}_i the machine-generated surrogate label, Ti∈{0,1}T_i \in \{0,1\} a treatment, and XiX_i covariates. The target is not prediction accuracy. It is the average treatment effect on the true outcome,

τ=𝔼[Yi(1)−Yi(0)].\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)].

But there could be failure modes. Surrogate-label error may be systematically related to treatment status or to treatment-induced features. When it is, plugging Ỹ\widetilde{Y} into a standard ATE estimator is biased, even when the surrogate is highly accurate overall.

The fix we develop is the design-based and prediction-powered idea. Here I use a small gold-labeled sample to estimate and remove that error, following Design-based Supervised Learning (DSL; Egami et al., 2023) and the prediction-powered inference family (Angelopoulos et al., 2023; Zrnic & Candès, 2024).

Why high accuracy is not enough

We write the surrogate error as the surrogate label minus the true outcome.

ei=Ỹi−Yi.e_i = \widetilde{Y}_i-Y_i.

Because Ỹ=Y+e\widetilde{Y}=Y+e, the naive difference in surrogate means decomposes exactly.

𝔼[Ỹ∣T=1]−𝔼[Ỹ∣T=0]⏟what the naive estimator targets=𝔼[Y∣T=1]−𝔼[Y∣T=0]⏟τ (under randomization)+𝔼[e∣T=1]−𝔼[e∣T=0]⏟B (differential error). \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Y}\mid T=1]-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Y}\mid T=0]}_{\text{what the naive estimator targets}} =\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y\mid T=1]-\mathbb{E}[Y\mid T=0]}_{\tau\text{ (under randomization)}} +\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[e\mid T=1]-\mathbb{E}[e\mid T=0]}_{B\text{ (differential error)}}.

The naive estimator is unbiased only when B=0B=0. The quantity that matters is not overall accuracy P(Ỹ=Y)P(\widetilde{Y}=Y) but whether the error is balanced across treatment arms. An LLM that is 90% accurate but slightly more likely to mislabel treated units has B≠0B\ne0 and biases τ\tau.

Egami et al. (2023) make this lesson precise. Surrogate accuracy of 80 to 90 percent can still produce substantial bias and invalid confidence intervals, because validity depends on the structure of the error, not its average size.

A map of the estimators

The estimators trade bias against variance (as the common bias-variance tradeoff in machine learning) and differ in what they assume about the error.

Estimator Uses Bias Variance Key assumption
Oracle (benchmark) YY for everyone none low simulation only
Gold-only YY on G=1G=1 low high (small nGn_G) gold sample representative
Naive surrogate Ỹ\widetilde{Y} for everyone BB low B=0B=0 (rarely holds)
Design-based correction (DSL, PPI) Ỹ\widetilde{Y} everywhere and YY on G=1G=1 low low to moderate known labeling prob. πi\pi_i

The last row is the one we want.

Doubly robust sense: the estimator stays consistent for τ\tau even if the prediction model is arbitrarily wrong, as long as the labeling probabilities are known. That guarantee is what DSL and prediction-powered inference share (Egami et al., 2023; Angelopoulos et al., 2023). They differ mainly in how the labeled sample is drawn and how the correction is written.

The correction, derived in steps

We build the design-based correction from scratch, then show the simple treatment-specific estimator is a special case, then call the dsl package.

Step 1 — labeling design. Let Ri=Gi∈{0,1}R_i=G_i\in\{0,1\} indicate that unit ii is gold-labeled, with known sampling probability πi=Pr⁡(Ri=1∣Xi,Ti)\pi_i=\Pr(R_i=1\mid X_i,T_i). Known is the crucial word. Because the analyst chooses who to label, πi\pi_i is a design quantity, not something to be estimated from a model.

Step 2 — prediction of the outcome. Fit any model m̂(Xi,Ỹi)\widehat{m}(X_i,\widetilde{Y}_i) for 𝔼[Y∣X,Ỹ]\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X,\widetilde{Y}] on the labeled data. It may use the surrogate, covariates, anything. It is allowed to be wrong.

Step 3 — bias-corrected pseudo-outcome. For every unit form

YiDSL=m̂(Xi,Ỹi)+Riπi(Yi−m̂(Xi,Ỹi)).Y_i^{\mathrm{DSL}}=\widehat{m}(X_i,\widetilde{Y}_i)+\frac{R_i}{\pi_i}\bigl(Y_i-\widehat{m}(X_i,\widetilde{Y}_i)\bigr).

The first term is available for everyone (always cheap predictions). The correction term is nonzero only on the gold sample and is inverse-probability weighted.

Step 4 — why it is unbiased. Since RiR_i is assigned by design with 𝔼[Ri/πi∣Xi,Ti,Yi,Ỹi]=1\mathbb{E}[R_i/\pi_i\mid X_i,T_i,Y_i,\widetilde{Y}_i]=1,

𝔼[YiDSL∣Xi,Ti]=m̂+𝔼[Yi−m̂]=𝔼[Yi∣Xi,Ti].\mathbb{E}[Y_i^{\mathrm{DSL}}\mid X_i,T_i]=\widehat{m}+\mathbb{E}[Y_i-\widehat{m}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_i\mid X_i,T_i].

The unknown m̂\widehat{m} cancels in expectation. A good m̂\widehat{m} does not buy correctness. However, it buys variance reduction, because the residual Y−m̂Y-\widehat{m} being corrected is small.

Step 5 — ATE. Plug the pseudo-outcome into the difference in means.

τ̂DSL=1n1∑i:Ti=1YiDSL−1n0∑i:Ti=0YiDSL.\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{DSL}}=\frac{1}{n_1}\sum_{i:T_i=1}Y_i^{\mathrm{DSL}}-\frac{1}{n_0}\sum_{i:T_i=0}Y_i^{\mathrm{DSL}}.

Step 6 — two familiar special cases that cause error

  • Treatment-specific correction. Take m̂(X,Ỹ)=Ỹ\widehat{m}(X,\widetilde{Y})=\widetilde{Y} and a constant π\pi. Then τ̂DSL=τ̂naive+(b̂1−b̂0)\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{DSL}}=\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{naive}}+(\widehat{b}_1-\widehat{b}_0), where b̂t=(Y−Ỹ)¯G=1,T=t\widehat{b}_t=\overline{(Y-\widetilde{Y})}_{G=1,T=t} estimates the per-arm error. This is the simplest possible debiasing.
  • Prediction-powered inference. With constant π=nG/n\pi=n_G/n, the formula equals predictions on all units plus a labeled rectifier, which is the PPI estimator of Angelopoulos et al. (2023). The efficient variant PPI++ tunes how much weight the predictions receive (Angelopoulos, Duchi, & Zrnic, 2023). DSL generalizes PPI to arbitrary, covariate-dependent labeling designs (Egami et al., 2023).

Setup: simulate a surrogate with controllable error structure

We generate randomized TT, a true binary YY, and a surrogate Ỹ\widetilde{Y} whose flip probability depends on treatment and covariates. The knobs let us turn the problem on and off.

Pr⁡(Ỹ≠Y∣T,X)=logit⁡−1(α0+αTT+αXX+αTXTX).\Pr(\widetilde{Y}\ne Y\mid T,X)=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\alpha_0+\alpha_T T+\alpha_X X+\alpha_{TX}TX).

We simulate the surrogate rather than calling a real LLM API, and this is deliberate. The estimator’s behavior depends only on the structure of the error e=Ỹ−Ye=\widetilde{Y}-Y, not on how the labels were produced. Simulation lets us set that structure and compare every estimator against a known ground truth, which a real API cannot give us. A real-data extension would replace the flip model with LLM labels and treat held-out human codes as YY. The lesson would be identical.

logit_inv <- function(z) 1 / (1 + exp(-z))

diff_means <- function(data, outcome) {
  data %>%
    group_by(T) %>%
    summarise(mu = mean(.data[[outcome]]), .groups = "drop") %>%
    summarise(ate = mu[T == 1] - mu[T == 0]) %>%
    pull(ate)
}

simulate_surrogate_data <- function(n = 4000, gold_n = 400,
                                    tau = 0.20,
                                    alpha_0 = -2.5, alpha_T = 0,
                                    alpha_X = 0, alpha_TX = 0,
                                    seed = NULL) {
  if (!is.null(seed)) set.seed(seed)
  X <- rnorm(n)
  T <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.5)                        # randomized treatment

  p_Y <- logit_inv(-0.3 + tau * T + 0.6 * X)          # true outcome model
  Y   <- rbinom(n, 1, p_Y)

  p_flip <- logit_inv(alpha_0 + alpha_T * T + alpha_X * X + alpha_TX * T * X)
  flip   <- rbinom(n, 1, p_flip)
  Y_tilde <- if_else(flip == 1, 1 - Y, Y)      # surrogate equals Y, sometimes flipped

  G <- rep(0, n)                                      # random gold sample, known pi = gold_n/n
  G[sample(seq_len(n), gold_n)] <- 1

  tibble(id = seq_len(n), X, T, Y, Y_tilde,
         error = Y_tilde - Y, flip,
         pi = gold_n / n, G)
}

Each function below is one row of the map above.

# Oracle. Simulation-only benchmark, uses true Y for everyone.
estimate_oracle <- function(data) diff_means(data, "Y")

# Gold-only. Uses Y but only on the validation sample.
estimate_gold_only <- function(data) {
  data %>% filter(G == 1) %>% diff_means("Y")
}

# Naive. Plugs the surrogate in for everyone. Biased when B is nonzero.
estimate_naive <- function(data) diff_means(data, "Y_tilde")

# Treatment-specific correction. m = Y_tilde, constant pi (Step 6, case 1).
estimate_treatment_corrected <- function(data) {
  b <- data %>% filter(G == 1) %>% group_by(T) %>%
    summarise(b_hat = mean(Y - Y_tilde), .groups = "drop")
  estimate_naive(data) +
    (b$b_hat[b$T == 1] - b$b_hat[b$T == 0])
}

# Design-based DSL correction. General m(X, Y_tilde), IPW correction (Steps 2 to 5).
estimate_dsl <- function(data, m_formula = I(Y) ~ T * X + Y_tilde) {
  fit <- lm(m_formula, data = filter(data, G == 1))   # Step 2, fit m on labeled
  data %>%
    mutate(
      m_hat = predict(fit, newdata = .),              # prediction for everyone
      Y_dsl = m_hat + (G / pi) * (Y - m_hat)          # Step 3, corrected pseudo-outcome
    ) %>%
    diff_means("Y_dsl")                               # Step 5, ATE on pseudo-outcome
}

Error cases of CI with surrogate labels

Scenario A. Error unrelated to treatment (αT=0\alpha_T=0)

Here the surrogate is noisy but its error is balanced across arms, so B=0B=0. The naive estimator should be roughly fine.

datA %>% group_by(T) %>%
  summarise(accuracy = mean(Y == Y_tilde),

As the results shows, naive estimate is the outlier. Both corrections pull back toward the oracle, and DSL does so at lower variance than gold-only because it borrows the full surrogate sample.

Using the real DSL package

The naive estimator above mirrors what dsl (Egami et al., 2023) does. The package adds valid standard errors and supports lm, logit, and felm. The workflow keeps the surrogate prediction for everyone, sets the gold label to NA where it is unobserved, and passes the known sampling probability.

# DSL wants predicted_var with NA on unlabeled rows, a prediction column for all,
# and the labeling probability. We reuse Scenario B.
dsl_df <- datB %>%
  transmute(
    Y           = if_else(G == 1, Y, NA_real_), # gold label, NA when not labeled
    pred_Y      = Y_tilde,                        # surrogate prediction, available for all
    T, X,
    sample_prob = pi
  )

library(dsl)

The coefficient on T in the table above is the DSL estimate of τ\tau. It lands close to the from-scratch estimate_dsl() point estimate and adds design-based standard errors and a confidence interval.

For other estimands the only change is model = "logit" or model = "felm" with fixed_effect, index, and cluster. The predicted_var argument may even be a right-hand-side variable, which is how DSL corrects mismeasured covariates, not just outcomes (Egami et al., 2023).

How far can the surrogate drift before it matters?

Sweep the treatment-correlated error strength αT\alpha_T and watch bias relative to the oracle. Accuracy stays high throughout, which is the whole point.

grid <- map_dfr(seq(0, 3, by = 0.25), function(aT) {
  d <- simulate_surrogate_data(alpha_T = aT, alpha_X = 0.4, alpha_TX = 0.4,
                               seed = 100 + round(aT * 10))
  oracle <- estimate_oracle(d)
  tibble(
    alpha_T = aT,
    accuracy = mean(d$Y == d$Y_tilde),
    naive_bias = estimate_naive(d) - oracle,
    tcorr_bias = estimate_treatment_corrected(d) - oracle,
    dsl_bias   = estimate_dsl(d) - oracle
  )
})
grid %>%
  select(alpha_T, naive_bias, tcorr_bias, dsl_bias) %>%
  pivot_longer(-alpha_T, names_to = "estimator", values_to = "bias") %>%
  ggplot(aes(alpha_T, bias, linetype = estimator)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0) +
  geom_line() + geom_point() +
  labs(x = "Strength of treatment-correlated surrogate error (alpha_T)",
       y = "Bias relative to oracle ATE",
       title = "Naive bias grows with treatment-correlated error, corrections stay near zero")
grid %>%
  ggplot(aes(alpha_T, accuracy)) +
  geom_line() + geom_point() +
  labs(x = "alpha_T", y = "Overall surrogate accuracy",
       title = "Accuracy barely moves, so it is not a sufficient diagnostic")

So far the gold sample is drawn at random, which fixes πi=nG/n\pi_i=n_G/n for everyone. When labels are expensive, a non-uniform design can do better. Choosing which units to label, and weighting by the known πi\pi_i, targets the estimand more efficiently. This is the active-inference and cross-prediction case of Zrnic and Candès (2024), and the general πi\pi_i in Step 3 already covers it. Plain random sampling is the safe default, and an active design is the upgrade when the labeling budget is the binding constraint.

Diagnostics on real data

In an application you never see YY outside the gold sample. Inside it you can check the things that actually govern validity. Report these alongside any accuracy number.

validation_diagnostics <- function(data) {
  gold <- data %>% filter(G == 1)
  list(
    by_treatment = gold %>% group_by(T) %>%
      summarise(accuracy = mean(Y_tilde == Y),
                signed_error = mean(Y_tilde - Y),   # is B nonzero?
                .groups = "drop"),
    error_model = tidy(lm(I(Y - Y_tilde) ~ T * X, data = gold)) # does error depend on T, X?
  )
}

A nonzero signed_error gap across treatment arms, or a significant T or T:X term in the error model, is direct evidence that the problem is present and that the naive estimate should not be trusted. This connects to a measurement question upstream of estimation. Whether the LLM applies the intended construct consistently across treatment arms and subgroups is itself a validity check (Halterman & Keith, 2024). When the model fails unevenly across arms, that unevenness is the treatment-correlated error, surfacing already at the measurement stage.

A short rule for applied work. If you have a pretrained labeler and can draw a random gold sample, plain DSL or PPI is enough. If labels are expensive and you can choose whom to label, an active design concentrates them where they cut variance most. In every case the gold sample must be informative about the error structure that enters the estimand. A representative random sample guarantees this. A convenience sample does not.
