1 Introduction and Goals

The statistical science community has gradually moved away from relying solely on fully parametrized statistical models. Traditional estimation and inference tools based on fully parametric models have been largely outdated. For instance, there is little justification to start with the belief that the data-generating process (DGP) is perfectly specified by a linear model and follows a Gaussian distribution N(μ,Σ)N(\mu,\Sigma). Instead, researchers are increasingly adopting semiparametric, or simply nonparametric, approaches in estimating and conducting causal inference on treatment and structural parameters of interest. Relying on much weaker assumptions about the true world, these new methods also have huge potential when integrated with machine learning (ML) tools, allowing for much more flexible estimators while preserving ideal asymptotic properties and robustness.

However, it has come to my attention that the required mathematical technicalities constituted a pedagogical barrier for the broader social science community. It is not uncommon to see the complicated mathematics and abstract notations impedes students’ reading and understanding of the literature in the field of semi-and nonparametric theory. This project serves as a “mathematical” tutorial oriented to a broader audience in the quantitative social science disciplines, aiming to provide a condensed reading guide to the classic theory in semi- and nonparametric estimation.

The following pages are divided as the following. A critical contribution of this project is Section 2, which features a collection of advanced mathematical tools and concepts frequently used in the semi-parametric theory literature. It mainly focuses on three areas, including functional analysis, likelihood and Fisher information, and a quick re-formulation of core concepts in probability theory via measure-theoretic language, which can be beneficial for understanding proofs. Section 3 is largely based on the review paper by Kennedy (2024). After a short recapture of parametric model, efficient estimator, and Cramér-Rao lower bound, we focus on the central idea of using submodels to derive the nonparametric equivalence of Cramér-Rao lower bound. Critical concepts like pathwise differentiability and influence function will be introduced along the way.

Section 4 draws heavily from the work by Chernozhukov et al. (2018) to introduce the powerful concept of Neyman orthogonality score, show how to construct orthogonal score function, and illustrate the usefulness of orthogonality via a worked out solution for estimating the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) using doubly-debiased estimator.

The main text of this tutorial focuses on building mathematical intuition and providing descriptive interpretation of abstract mathematical concepts. Although theorems and definitions are going to be formally stated, proofs and mathematical rigor are of secondary considerations. The Appendix collects proofs of some important results and mathematical facts. Mathematically-oriented readers are encouraged to use it as a reference.

2 Preliminary Mathematics and Notations

2.1 Functional Analysis

Many estimands of interest–usually some sort of conditional expectation functions– often take in a function as an input and evaluates to a real number. To facilitate the discussion of semi-parametric estimation and Neyman orthogonality, we first provide a crash course on functionals and functional derivatives.

Definition 1. A functional defined most generally is a mapping:

Ψ:𝒳→𝒴, \Psi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y},

2.2 Measure-based Probability Theory

Definition 2 (Probability Space). Given a generic sample space Ω\Omega, we endow it with a σ\sigma-algebra ℱ\mathcal{F}. Define a probability measure ℙ:ℱ→[0,1]\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]. We call the triple (Ω,ℱ,ℙ)(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}) a probability space. A measure space is a more general triple of the form (X,𝒮,μ)(X,\mathcal{S},\mu).

Definition 3 (Random Variables, Expectation, and Variance). Given a probability space (Ω,ℱ,ℙ)(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}), a random variable XX is an ℱ−\mathcal{F}- measurable function: X:Ω→ℝX:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}. A realization of the random variable is X(ω)=x∈ℝ,ω∈ΩX(\omega)=x\in\mathbb{R},\omega\in\Omega. The expectation and variance of XX are defined as the Lebesgue integrals:

𝔼ℙ[X]=∫ΩXdℙ(ω),Var[X]=𝔼[(X−𝔼[X])2].(4) \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[X]=\int_\Omega X\,d\mathbb{P}(\omega),\quad Var[X]=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2].\qquad (4)

We note that if there exists a density function p(x)p(x) for ℙ\mathbb{P} such that dℙ=pd\mathbb{P}=p, the expectation can be equivalently characterized by the usual Riemann integral:

𝔼[X]=∫ℝxp(x)dλ(x), \mathbb{E}[X]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,p(x)\,d\lambda(x),

where λ(⋅)\lambda(\cdot) is the Lebesgue measure on the real numbers.

2.3 Likelihood Theory and Fisher Information

Lastly, since many score functions attain the form of log-likelihood or score function, we specify a few definitions and two useful theorems related to likelihood theory. In this subsection, let z1,z2,⋯,zn∼i.i.dPθz_1,z_2,\cdots,z_n\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}P_\theta, where {Pθ:θ∈Θ⊂ℝd}\{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^d\} is a model space with well-defined probability densities {pθ(zi)}i=1n\{p_\theta(z_i)\}_{i=1}^n. ℓ(θ)=log⁡pθ(zi).(10) \ell(\theta)=\log p_\theta(z_i).\qquad (10)

θ̂n∈arg⁡maxθ∈ΘLn(θ)=arg⁡maxθ∈Θℓn(θ), \hat\theta_n\in\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\arg\max}\ L_n(\theta)=\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\arg\max}\ \ell_n(\theta),

where In(θ)I_n(\theta) is the Fisher information in the sample:

In(θ)=n⋅I(θ). I_n(\theta)=n\cdot I(\theta).

Visually, consider a uni-dimensional parameter setting. More information means the likelihood is more sharply curved around the truth.

3 From Parametric to Nonparametric Estimation

3.1 A Quick Recap of Smooth Parametric Model Estimation

In the parametric context, our model space is a collection 𝒫={Pθ:θ∈Θ}\mathcal{P}=\{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}. For demonstrative purposes, we consider Θ⊂ℝ\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}. Suppose we have a target to learn, expressed as a functional ψ(θ)\psi(\theta). We estimate ψ̂\hat\psi from data ZZ. The core issue this section deals with is finding a benchmark for the efficiency of different estimators ψ̂\hat\psi, defined in terms of Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss. A classical result for smooth parametric models is the Crámer–Rao lower bound.

Suppose that both PθP_\theta and ψ(θ)\psi(\theta) are differentiable in θ\theta. Then for an unbiased estimator ψ̂\hat\psi of ψ(θ)\psi(\theta),

Var⁡(ψ̂)≥(ψ′(θ))2Var⁡(Sθ(Z)),(16) \operatorname{Var}(\hat\psi)\geq\frac{\bigl(\psi'(\theta)\bigr)^2}{\operatorname{Var}\bigl(S_\theta(Z)\bigr)},\qquad (16)

where

ψ′(θ)=∂∂θψ(θ)andSθ(z)=∂∂θlog⁡pθ(z)(17) \psi'(\theta)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\psi(\theta)\quad\text{and}\quad S_\theta(z)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log p_\theta(z)\qquad (17)

is the score function. In this expression, Var⁡(Sθ(Z))\operatorname{Var}\bigl(S_\theta(Z)\bigr) is the Fisher information, and the bound identifies the smallest achievable variance among unbiased estimators of ψ(θ)\psi(\theta).

More generally, one can benchmark an estimator through a common trick of minimax lower bound (Kennedy, 2024, 6):

infψ̂supP∈𝒫𝔼P[(ψ̂−ψ(P))2]≥Rn. \inf_{\hat\psi}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_P\left[\bigl(\hat\psi-\psi(P)\bigr)^2\right]\geq R_n.

Taking the first layer of supremum takes the worst-case mean squared error over the entire model class 𝒫\mathcal{P}, then taking the infimum select one estimator out of all candidate estimators that minimizes the worst-case MSE.

A natural question to ask, then, is can the idea of Cramér–Rao lower bound (16) and parametric efficiency be generalized to the nonparametric setting?

3.2 Parametric Submodels

Now we are in the case where the model space is much more flexible. Namely, 𝒫\mathcal{P} can contain any well-defined probability distribution function PP, which makes it more challenging to find the target ψ(ℙ)\psi(\mathbb{P}), where ℙ\mathbb{P} denotes the true probability measure, since we have to travel through a much larger space. How do we connect the Cramér–Rao lower bound to semi- or non-parametric models? The key is to invoke the idea parametric submodels.

Smooth submodels Let the full model be 𝒫\mathcal{P}, and consider a smooth one-dimensional submodel

𝒫ε={Pε:ε∈ℝ} \mathcal{P}_\varepsilon=\{P_\varepsilon:\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}\}

such that

𝒫ε⊆𝒫,Pε=0=P.(18) \mathcal{P}_\varepsilon\subseteq\mathcal{P},\qquad P_{\varepsilon=0}=P.\qquad (18)

What 18 means is that the submodels is contained in the overarching model space and hence is easier to travel through. Also, when evaluating ε\varepsilon at zero, we recover the true probability distribution. Then we estimate the target ψ(ℙ)\psi(\mathbb{P}) by studying it along the smaller model 𝒫ε\mathcal{P}_\varepsilon.

Non-parametric model via a submodel For a non-parametric model 𝒫\mathcal{P}, we usually construct a one-parameter submodel using:

dPε=dℙ(z){1+εh(z)},(19) dP_\varepsilon=d\mathbb{P}(z)\{1+\varepsilon h(z)\},\qquad (19)

where h:Z→ℝh:Z\to\mathbb{R} is a mean-zero function satisfying

‖h‖∞<∞,|ε| small enough so that pε(z)≥0. \lVert h\rVert_\infty<\infty,\qquad |\varepsilon|\text{ small enough so that }p_\varepsilon(z)\geq0.

Construction (19) represents a local hh−perturbation away from a nonparametric model P∈𝒫P\in\mathcal P, controlled by ε\varepsilon. What’s more, we notice that the corresponding density pεp_\varepsilon of the perturbed probability distribution PεP_\varepsilon has the following nice property:

∂∂εlogpε(z)|ε=0=∂∂εlog(1+εh(z))|ε=0=h(z)1+εh(z)|ε=0=h(z),(20) \left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\log p_\varepsilon(z)\right|_{\varepsilon=0} =\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\log\bigl(1+\varepsilon h(z)\bigr)\right|_{\varepsilon=0} =\left.\frac{h(z)}{1+\varepsilon h(z)}\right|_{\varepsilon=0} =h(z),\qquad (20)

meaning the score for this submodel at ε=0\varepsilon=0 is exactly h(z)h(z).

To examine how local perturbation of probability measure impact the learning of ψ(P)\psi(P), we invoke the following distributional analog of Taylor expansion.

Definition 8 (von Mises expansion). For a smooth functional ψ:𝒫→ℝ\psi:\mathcal P\to\mathbb R, define the distributional Taylor expansion by

ψ(P‾)−ψ(P)=∫φ(z;P‾)d(P‾−P)(z)+R2(P‾,P),(21) \psi(\bar P)-\psi(P)=\int\varphi(z;\bar P)\,d(\bar P-P)(z)+R_2(\bar P,P),\qquad (21)

for P‾,P∈𝒫\bar P,P\in\mathcal P, where

∫φ(z;P)dP(z)=0,∫φ(z;P)2dP(z)<∞. \int\varphi(z;P)\,dP(z)=0,\qquad \int\varphi(z;P)^2\,dP(z)<\infty.

The smoothness of the submodel and expansion (21) give rise to the following crucial result in semi- and nonparametric efficient estimation theory: pathwise differentiability.

A functional ψ:𝒫→ℝ\psi:\mathcal P\to\mathbb R is said to be pathwise differentiable at PP if for every regular parametric submodel PεP_\varepsilon

ddεψ(Pε)|ε=0=∫φ(z;ℙ)Sε(z)dℙ(z),(22) \left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\psi(P_\varepsilon)\right|_{\varepsilon=0} =\int\varphi(z;\mathbb P)\,S_\varepsilon(z)\,d\mathbb P(z),\qquad (22)

where the function φ\varphi is often called the influence function, as it quantifies exactly how the local perturbation via the submodel influences the target. A full proof to statement (22) can be found in Appendix A.

3.3 Pathwise Differentiability and Generalization of Cramér–Rao bound

Here, we show that the detour we went through above can be used to generalize the Cramér–Rao bound, via pathwise differentiability.

First observe that the Cramér–Rao lower bound for a given Pε∈𝒫εP_\varepsilon\in\mathcal P_\varepsilon is

ψ′(Pε)2Var⁡Pε(Sε(Z))=(∂∂εψ(Pε))2|ε=0𝔼[h(Z)2]=𝔼[φ(z,ℙ)h(z)]2𝔼[h(Z)2], \frac{\psi'(P_\varepsilon)^2}{\operatorname{Var}_{P_\varepsilon}(S_\varepsilon(Z))} =\frac{\left.\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\psi(P_\varepsilon)\right)^2\right|_{\varepsilon=0}}{\mathbb E[h(Z)^2]} =\frac{\mathbb E[\varphi(z,\mathbb P)h(z)]^2}{\mathbb E[h(Z)^2]},

where the first equality follows from definition and i.i.d assumption, and the second equality follows from (22), specifically

∫φ(z;ℙ)Sε(z)dℙ(z)=𝔼[φ(z,ℙ)h(z)]. \int\varphi(z;\mathbb P)\,S_\varepsilon(z)\,d\mathbb P(z)=\mathbb E[\varphi(z,\mathbb P)h(z)].

Then over all the Cramér–Rao bound at ε=0\varepsilon=0, we simply take the supremum of all bounds:

supPεψ′(Pε)2Var⁡Pε(Sε(Z))=suph𝔼[φ(z,ℙ)h(z)]2𝔼[h(z)2].(23) \sup_{P_\varepsilon}\frac{\psi'(P_\varepsilon)^2}{\operatorname{Var}_{P_\varepsilon}(S_\varepsilon(Z))} =\sup_h\frac{\mathbb E[\varphi(z,\mathbb P)h(z)]^2}{\mathbb E[h(z)^2]}.\qquad (23)

Notice, by the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality (9), for each hh, we have:

𝔼[φ(z,ℙ)h(z)]2≤𝔼[φ(z,ℙ)2]⋅𝔼[h(z)2],(24) \mathbb E[\varphi(z,\mathbb P)h(z)]^2\leq\mathbb E[\varphi(z,\mathbb P)^2]\cdot\mathbb E[h(z)^2],\qquad (24)

which implies ∀h\forall h,

𝔼[φ(z,ℙ)h(z)]2𝔼[h(z)2]≤𝔼[φ(z,ℙ)2]=Var⁡[φ(z)]. \frac{\mathbb E[\varphi(z,\mathbb P)h(z)]^2}{\mathbb E[h(z)^2]} \leq\mathbb E[\varphi(z,\mathbb P)^2]=\operatorname{Var}[\varphi(z)].

In another word, we have an upper-bound for 𝔼[φ(z,ℙ)h(z)]2𝔼[h(z)2]\frac{\mathbb E[\varphi(z,\mathbb P)h(z)]^2}{\mathbb E[h(z)^2]}. Since supremum is the least upper-bound, then (23) becomes

supPεψ′(Pε)2Var⁡Pε(Sε(Z))=suph𝔼[φ(z,ℙ)h(z)]2𝔼[h(z)2]≤Var⁡[φ(z)](25) \sup_{P_\varepsilon}\frac{\psi'(P_\varepsilon)^2}{\operatorname{Var}_{P_\varepsilon}(S_\varepsilon(Z))} =\sup_h\frac{\mathbb E[\varphi(z,\mathbb P)h(z)]^2}{\mathbb E[h(z)^2]} \leq\operatorname{Var}[\varphi(z)]\qquad (25)

Finally, we now can assert that for an estimator ψ̂\hat\psi, it satisfies the nonparametric efficiency bound if

n(ψ̂−ψ)→dN(0,Var⁡[φ(z)]). \sqrt n(\hat\psi-\psi)\xrightarrow{d}N\bigl(0,\operatorname{Var}[\varphi(z)]\bigr).

4 Neyman Orthogonality and Double/Debiased Machine Learning Estimator

So far we have looked at fully parametrized models and nonparametric models, which are two extreme cases. In the former, we as researchers have a perfectly informative prior that the state of the world works exactly according to our model specification, while in the latter we do not impose any prior beliefs and assumptions about the data-generating process. With the popularization of machine learning tools, nonparametric inference also has become a conventional tool when the researchers are not sure how control variables should enter the model, which allows flexible model forms (e.g., the regression function Y=g(X)+UY=g(X)+U does not necessarily have g(X)=X′βg(X)=X'\beta).

Drawing from Chernozhukov et al. (2018), this last chapter presents the intermediate approach: semiparametric estimation. We are interested in the setting where our goal is to estimate a low-dimensional parameter θ0\theta_0, but we are dealing with a high-dimensional nuisance η0\eta_0 (e.g., regression adjustment or the propensity score).

The key challenge here is that ML methods applied to learn η0\eta_0 is to use regularization bias to trade off high variance in estimator θ̂0\hat\theta_0 of θ0\theta_0, but the bias in learning the nuisance parameters also induces bias in θ̂0\hat\theta_0, which implies we have a situation where the estimator is not centered at θ0\theta_0 and not n\sqrt n consistent:

n(θ̂0−θ0)→p∞.(26) \sqrt n(\hat\theta_0-\theta_0)\xrightarrow{p}\infty.\qquad (26)

The solutions are two simple but powerful fixes: 1. Neyman Orthogonal Scores and 2. Cross-fitting. The resulting estimators resulting from those procedures are known as Double/Debiased ML estimators, which are unbiased and enjoy the n\sqrt n consistency.

4.1 Definition and Set-up of Neyman Orthogonality

Neyman Orthogonality Condition We set up the Neyman Orthogonality condition. We have a θ0∈Θ≠⌀\theta_0\in\Theta\neq\varnothing, Θ⊂ℝdθ\Theta\subset\mathbb R^{d_\theta}. The true parameter θ0\theta_0 satisfies the moment condition

𝔼P[ψ(W;θ0,η0)]=0,(27) \mathbb E_P[\psi(W;\theta_0,\eta_0)]=0,\qquad (27)

where ψ=(ψ1,ψ2,⋯,ψdθ)′\psi=(\psi_1,\psi_2,\cdots,\psi_{d_\theta})' is the vector of the score functions, WW is the random variable in the probability space (𝒲,𝒜W,P)(\mathcal W,\mathcal A_W,P), P∈PNP\in P_N is a generic probability measure, η0\eta_0 is the true value of nuisance parameter η∈T\eta\in T, TT is a convex subset of some normed vector space endowed with norm ‖⋅‖T\lVert\cdot\rVert_T.

We further assume each score function ψj:𝒲×Θ×T→ℝ\psi_j:\mathcal W\times\Theta\times T\to\mathbb R is also measurable once we endow a Borel σ\sigma−field on Θ\Theta and TT respectively. Let a sample of observations (Wi)i=1N(W_i)_{i=1}^N be given.

Denote the shifted set of nuisance realizations T̃={η−η0:η∈T}\tilde T=\{\eta-\eta_0:\eta\in T\}, we define the pathwise (Grat́eaux) derivative as a mapping: Dr:T̃→ℝdθD_r:\tilde T\to\mathbb R^{d_\theta}

Dr[η−η0]=∂r{𝔼P[ψ(W;θ0,η0+r(η−η0))],η∈T},(28) D_r[\eta-\eta_0]=\partial_r\{\mathbb E_P[\psi(W;\theta_0,\eta_0+r(\eta-\eta_0))],\ \eta\in T\},\qquad (28)

for all r∈[0,1)r\in[0,1). Note this rr introduced is similar to the idea of model path. Also note that ψ(W;θ0,η0+r(η−η0))\psi(W;\theta_0,\eta_0+r(\eta-\eta_0)) is well-defined because η0+r(η−η0)=(1−r)η0−rη\eta_0+r(\eta-\eta_0)=(1-r)\eta_0-r\eta is a convex combination of two elements in the convex set TT, so η0+r(η−η0)∈T\eta_0+r(\eta-\eta_0)\in T.

For convenience, instead of using the path induced by rr, we define:

∂η𝔼P[ψ(W;θ0,η0)][η−η0]:=D0[η−η0],η∈T(29) \partial_\eta\mathbb E_P[\psi(W;\theta_0,\eta_0)][\eta-\eta_0]:=D_0[\eta-\eta_0],\qquad\eta\in T\qquad (29)

Denote 𝒯N⊂T\mathcal T_N\subset T as a set of nuisance realizations such that the estimators η̂0\hat\eta_0 of η\eta specified below take values in this set with high probability. In practice, we typically assume 𝒯N\mathcal T_N is a properly shrinking neighborhood of TT. The Neyman Orthogonality Condition is essentially the derivative (29) vanishes for all η∈𝒯N\eta\in\mathcal T_N.

Definition 9 (Neyman Orthogonality). The score vector ψ=(ψ1,⋯,ψdθ)′\psi=(\psi_1,\cdots,\psi_{d_\theta})' obeys the orthogonality condition at (θ0,η0)(\theta_0,\eta_0) with respect to 𝒯N⊂T\mathcal T_N\subset T if moment condition (27) holds and the pathwise derivative Dr[η−η0]D_r[\eta-\eta_0] exists for all r∈[0,1)r\in[0,1) and all η∈𝒯N\eta\in\mathcal T_N and vanishes at r=0r=0, namely

∂η𝔼P[ψ(W;θ0,η0)][η−η0]=0,∀η∈𝒯N(30) \partial_\eta\mathbb E_P[\psi(W;\theta_0,\eta_0)][\eta-\eta_0]=0,\ \forall\eta\in\mathcal T_N\qquad (30)

We remark that the condition (30) will be satisfied with 𝒯N=T\mathcal T_N=T when η\eta is finite dimensional as long as

∂η𝔼P[ψj(W;θ0,η0)]=0,∀j=1,2,⋯,dθ, \partial_\eta\mathbb E_P[\psi_j(W;\theta_0,\eta_0)]=0,\ \forall j=1,2,\cdots,d_\theta,

where ∂η𝔼P[ψj(W;θ0,η0)]\partial_\eta\mathbb E_P[\psi_j(W;\theta_0,\eta_0)] denotes the vector of partial derivatives of the function η↦EP[ψj(W;θ0,η)]\eta\mapsto E_P[\psi_j(W;\theta_0,\eta)] evaluated at η=η0\eta=\eta_0.

4.2 Construction of Orthogonal Score

Section 4.1 can feel too technical and irrelevant to research practice, so this section first provides motivation for using Neyman Orthogonal score to learn the target parameter and constructing one if the original natural score is not orthogonal. Second, we describe the process of orthogonalizing a score function using a toy example of log-likelihood function and ties it back to the idea that using doubly robust estimator is considered as a superior approach in causal inference.

4.2.1 Motivation: Why orthogonality?

Recall that in semiparametric inference, we have to start from some identification assumption, which is often formed as some sort of GMM-style moment condition:

𝔼[ψ(W;θ0,η0)]=0, \mathbb E[\psi(W;\theta_0,\eta_0)]=0,

where θ\theta is the target parameter and η\eta is the nuisance function. Since it is the population statement, with data {Wi}i=1n\{W_i\}_{i=1}^n, we solve the moment condition in its sample analog. Then the estimator θ̂\hat\theta is the solution to

1n∑i=1nψ(Wi;θ̂,η̂)=0,(31) \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\psi(W_i;\hat\theta,\hat\eta)=0,\qquad (31)

where η̂\hat\eta is learned from data either parametrically or flexibly using machine learning. The core challenge we are dealing with, to reiterate, is that the regularization bias induced by estimating η̂\hat\eta propagates in the asymptotic distribution of θ̂\hat\theta. To see this, consider the Taylor expansion of sample moment condition (31) around the truth (θ0,η0)(\theta_0,\eta_0):

0=1n∑i=1nψ(Wi;θ0,η0)+1n∑i=1n∂θψ(Wi;θ0,η0)⏟:−Jn(θ̂−θ0)+1n∑i=1n∂ηψ(Wi;θ0,η0)(η̂−η0)+o(‖η̂−η0‖2)⏟higher order terms⇒−Jn(θ̂−θ0)=1n∑i=1nψ(Wi;θ0,η0)+1n∑i=1n∂ηψ(Wi;θ0,η0)(η̂−η0)+o(‖η̂−η0‖2)solve for: (θ̂−θ0)=−Jn−1(1n∑i=1nψ(Wi;θ0,η0)+1n∑i=1n∂ηψ(Wi;θ0,η0)(η̂−η0)+o(‖η̂−η0‖2)) \begin{aligned} 0={}&\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0) +\underbrace{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\partial_\theta\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0)}_{:-J_n}(\hat\theta-\theta_0)+{}\\ &\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\partial_\eta\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0)(\hat\eta-\eta_0) +\underbrace{o(\lVert\hat\eta-\eta_0\rVert^2)}_{\text{higher order terms}}\\ \Longrightarrow\ -J_n(\hat\theta-\theta_0)={}&\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0) +\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\partial_\eta\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0)(\hat\eta-\eta_0) +o(\lVert\hat\eta-\eta_0\rVert^2)\\ \text{solve for: }(\hat\theta-\theta_0)={}&-J_n^{-1}\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0) +\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\partial_\eta\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0)(\hat\eta-\eta_0) +o(\lVert\hat\eta-\eta_0\rVert^2)\right) \end{aligned}

Scaling the last line of result above by n\sqrt n and examining the asymptotic behavior of n(θ̂−θ0)\sqrt n(\hat\theta-\theta_0), we can quickly see it does not enjoy nice asymptotic properties:

n(θ̂−θ0)=−Jn−1(1n∑i=1nψ(Wi;θ0,η0)⏟CLT+1n∑i=1n∂ηψ(Wi;θ0,η0)(η̂−η0)⏟Trouble Term+no(‖η̂−η0‖2)),(32) \sqrt n(\hat\theta-\theta_0)=-J_n^{-1}\left( \underbrace{\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0)}_{CLT} +\underbrace{\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\partial_\eta\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0)(\hat\eta-\eta_0)}_{\text{Trouble Term}} +\sqrt n o(\lVert\hat\eta-\eta_0\rVert^2) \right),\qquad (32)

where the first CLT labeled term enjoys a Central Limit Theorem since it is just a sample average, implied by the law of large numbers and continuous mapping theorem, and the higher order term can be neglected under some structural assumptions (e.g., sparsity or Lasso penalty). The Trouble Term in (32) is problematic due to the following two reasons.

Regularization Bias: neither ∂ηψ(Wi;θ0,η0)\partial_\eta\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0) nor η̂−η0\hat\eta-\eta_0 has mean zero in general, which means this whole term will not be properly centered asymptotically.

Overfitting Bias: we cannot assume ∂ηψ(Wi;θ0,η0)⟂η̂−η0\partial_\eta\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0)\perp\hat\eta-\eta_0 because η̂\hat\eta is learned from WiW_i’s, so we cannot split ∂ηψ\partial_\eta\psi and η̂−η0\hat\eta-\eta_0 in expectation operator. More precisely, even if each individual term ∂ηψ\partial_\eta\psi and η̂−η0\hat\eta-\eta_0 is mean zero, we still might have 𝔼[∂ηψ(η̂−η0)]≠0\mathbb E[\partial_\eta\psi(\hat\eta-\eta_0)]\neq0. This is where the Neyman Orthogonality in Section 4.1 comes to rescue.

Neyman Orthogonal Scores kills regularization bias: Observe that imposing the Neyman Orthogonality condition in (30) mechanically forces the ∂ηψ(Wi;θ0,η0)\partial_\eta\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0) term in (32) to be zero mean. In this view, (30) simply means that the first-order influence of bias in learning the nuisance parameter η̂\hat\eta does not propagate to the learning of the distribution of θ̂\hat\theta. We ignore the presence of second and higher-order influence of regularization bias.

Cross-fitting addresses overfitting bias: We split the data {Wi}i=1n\{W_i\}_{i=1}^n into KK folds and using parts of the data to learn η̂\hat\eta and then use other parts to learn ψ(Wi;θ̂,η̂)\psi(W_i;\hat\theta,\hat\eta), which gives independence.

The second ingredient can be implemented via off-the-shelf ML methods, and the next order of business is to make sure we can actually find an orthogonal score function ψ\psi.

4.2.2 General Construction of Neyman Orthogonal Scores

Section 4.2.1 motivates the use of an orthogonal score, but it is likely that the original score function that the target parameter of interest admits is NOT orthogonal. Section 4.2.2 offers some reassurance that one can generally construct an orthogonal score based on the original score function for most of the causal parameters common in social scientific research.

Let an original score φ\varphi be given, and it does not satisfy the orthogonality condition above, our goal is to orthogonalize it into ψ\psi. To explain this better, we discuss the Neyman orthogonal scores for likelihood and finite-dimensional nuisance parameters. For other approaches, such as the centering-out, see Chernozhukov et al. (2018).

We play with a toy example by taking ℓ(W;θ,β)\ell(W;\theta,\beta) to be a criterion function, typically a log-likelihood or quasi-log-likelihood, where θ∈Θ⊂ℝdθ\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb R^{d_\theta}, for a convext set ℬ\mathcal B, is the target parameter and β∈ℬ⊂ℝdβ\beta\in\mathcal B\subset\mathbb R^{d_\beta} is the nuisance parameter. The true parameters (θ0,β0)(\theta_0,\beta_0) solves the familiar optimization question:

maxθ∈Θ,β∈ℬ𝔼[l(W;θ,β)](33) \max_{\theta\in\Theta,\beta\in\mathcal B}\mathbb E[l(W;\theta,\beta)]\qquad (33)

The original score for θ\theta is defined literally as the score function for log-likelihood as (12) Section 2:

φ(W;θ,β)=∂θℓ(W;θ,β). \varphi(W;\theta,\beta)=\partial_\theta\ell(W;\theta,\beta).

Notice (33) implies the moment conditions at the truth are satisfied:

𝔼[∂θl(W;θ0,β0)]=0,𝔼[∂βl(W;θ0,β0)]=0.(34) \mathbb E[\partial_\theta l(W;\theta_0,\beta_0)]=0,\quad \mathbb E[\partial_\beta l(W;\theta_0,\beta_0)]=0.\qquad (34)

So in our case the identifying moment condition (27) checks out. However, this score is generally not orthogonal to the nuisance parameter β\beta, namely we generally have

∂β𝔼[∂θl(W;θ0,β0)]≠0, \partial_\beta\mathbb E[\partial_\theta l(W;\theta_0,\beta_0)]\neq0,

so estimation error in β\beta may affect inference on θ\theta at first order.

To remove this sensitivity, one constructs the orthogonal score

ψ(W;θ,η)=∂θℓ(W;θ,β)−μ∂βℓ(W;θ,β),(35) \psi(W;\theta,\eta)=\partial_\theta\ell(W;\theta,\beta)-\mu\,\partial_\beta\ell(W;\theta,\beta),\qquad (35)

where the nuisance parameter vector is extended from just β′\beta' to η=(β′,vec⁡(μ)′)′∈ℬ×ℝdθdβ\eta=(\beta',\operatorname{vec}(\mu)')'\in\mathcal B\times\mathbb R^{d_\theta d_\beta} and the matrix μ\mu is an orthogonalization matrix so that the constructed score (35) becomes orthogonal to perturbations in the nuisance direction. We now unpack a little how to solve for this μ\mu.

The key is to back-engineer: we simply need to find a μ\mu such that the constructed new score (35) is now orthogonal to influence caused by the nuisance β\beta. Taking the partial derivative with respect β\beta and centering around the truth gives:

∂βψ=∂β∂θℓ−μ∂β2ℓ=0.(36) \partial_\beta\psi=\partial_\beta\partial_\theta\ell-\mu\partial_\beta^2\ell=0.\qquad (36)

Let JθβJ_{\theta\beta} denotes the matrix of ∂(θ′,β′)′\partial_{(\theta',\beta')'} valuated at (α0,β0)(\alpha_0,\beta_0), and JββJ_{\beta\beta} defined similarly, we notice they are just submatrices in the Hessian matrix of the objective function:

J=∂(θ′,β′)′𝔼[∂(θ′,β′)′ℓ(W;θ,β)]|θ=θ0,β=β0=𝔼[∂(θ′,β′)′2ℓ(W;θ,β)]|θ=θ0,β=β0.(37) J=\left.\partial_{(\theta',\beta')'}\mathbb E\left[\partial_{(\theta',\beta')'}\ell(W;\theta,\beta)\right]\right|_{\theta=\theta_0,\beta=\beta_0} =\left.\mathbb E\left[\partial_{(\theta',\beta')'}^2\ell(W;\theta,\beta)\right]\right|_{\theta=\theta_0,\beta=\beta_0}.\qquad (37)

To be more transparent, the four symmetric blocks in JJ are the corresponding submatrices in the form below

J=(∂θ𝔼[∂θℓ]∂β𝔼[∂θℓ]∂θ𝔼[∂βℓ]∂β𝔼[∂βℓ])(θ,β)=(θ0,β0)=(JθθJθβJβθJββ). J=\begin{pmatrix} \partial_\theta\mathbb E[\partial_\theta\ell]&\partial_\beta\mathbb E[\partial_\theta\ell]\\[6pt] \partial_\theta\mathbb E[\partial_\beta\ell]&\partial_\beta\mathbb E[\partial_\beta\ell] \end{pmatrix}_{(\theta,\beta)=(\theta_0,\beta_0)} =\begin{pmatrix}J_{\theta\theta}&J_{\theta\beta}\\J_{\beta\theta}&J_{\beta\beta}\end{pmatrix}.

Lastly, (36) implies the orthogonality condition is reduced to solving

Jθβ−μJββ=0, J_{\theta\beta}-\mu J_{\beta\beta}=0,

which yields the solution

μ=JθβJββ−1, \mu=J_{\theta\beta}J_{\beta\beta}^{-1},

assuming Jββ−1J_{\beta\beta}^{-1} is full-rank. If this construction using the log-likelihood feels still too technical, Appendix B works through the above procedure in the high dimensional regression setting, where the familiar linear model specification can help with understanding the mathematics.

Similar procedure extends immediately beyond the case of a criterion function. For instance, Chernozhukov et al. (2018) also discusses the construction for GMM settings where the moment condition is more general 𝔼[m(W,θ0,β0)]=0\mathbb E[m(W,\theta_0,\beta_0)]=0, but we omit its derivation due to complex matrix algebra.

4.3 Illustration via Estimation of ATE

Two takeaways from the exercises in Section 4.2 are in order. First, Neyman Orthogonality mechanically ensures that the regularization bias does not propagate in the process of learning the target parameter in first order. Second, for any original, not necessarily orthogonal score φ\varphi, we can construct a new orthogonal score by extending the nuisance parameter β∈ℬ\beta\in\mathcal B to η∈𝒯\eta\in\mathcal T, where 𝒯\mathcal T is usually a superset of ℬ\mathcal B. The second point seems a little counterintuitive, as we are introducing more nuisance elements into estimation. This section aims to elucidate this idea by working through the case of ATE, which is one of the most common causal targets in social scientific inquiry. At the end of this chapter, we hope the readers will see the process of building up to an orthogonal score is the same as finding a “doubly robust” estimator. The extra nuisance parameters are brought in exactly to offset potential errors in learning existing nuisance parameters and ensure such bias does not propagate in the learning of the causal target.

Most generally, the idea is that given φ(W;θ,β)\varphi(W;\theta,\beta), we can almost always find an adjustment ϕ(W;θ,η)\phi(W;\theta,\eta) such that

ψ(W;θ,η)=φ(W;θ,β)+ϕ(W;θ,η), \psi(W;\theta,\eta)=\varphi(W;\theta,\beta)+\phi(W;\theta,\eta),

as long as ϕ\phi satisfy some nice conditions (Chernozhukov et al., 2018). Table 2 collects the constructed Neyman orthogonal scores for other common causal targets.

For our example, we focus on the ATE, identifiable via

α:=ATE=𝔼[𝔼[Y(1)−Y(0)]∣X], \alpha:=\mathrm{ATE}=\mathbb E[\mathbb E[Y(1)-Y(0)]\mid X],

where we assume the treatment DD is binary and XX are given as controls.

Regression Adjustment Estimator. Since we are given controls, one standard approach is to estimate the ATE via regression adjustment:

ATÊreg=1n∑i=1n(ĝ1(xi)−ĝ0(xi)),(38) \widehat{\mathrm{ATE}}_{\mathrm{reg}}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\bigl(\hat g_1(x_i)-\hat g_0(x_i)\bigr),\qquad (38)

where g1(X)=Y(1)g_1(X)=Y(1) and g0(X)=Y(0)g_0(X)=Y(0) represent the true regression equation when the treatment is turned on and off, respectively. Is this a good estimator? It will be consistent fairly generally as long as regression functions flexibly estimated, but it will NOT be generally asymptotically normal unless estimators for g1(⋅)g_1(\cdot) and g0(⋅)g_0(\cdot) are correctly specified parametric models.

For instance, If one specifies linear models g0(x)=x′β0g_0(x)=x'\beta_0 and g1(x)=x′β1g_1(x)=x'\beta_1, then ATÊreg\widehat{\mathrm{ATE}}_{\mathrm{reg}} is equivalent to coefficient on DD from regression of YY on DD, XX, and D(X−X‾)D(X-\bar X). The usual OLS standard errors ignore sampling uncertainty from using X‾\bar X in place of 𝔼[X]\mathbb E[X]. In another word, it is too strong to assume X‾→p𝔼[X]\bar X\xrightarrow{p}\mathbb E[X].

What’s more, writing in the framework of score function ψ\psi, we are trying to find solution to the moment condition

𝔼[ψreg(W;α0,h0)]=𝔼[g1(X)−g0(X)−α]=0,ψreg(W;α0,h0)=g1(X)−g0(X)−α,(39) \mathbb E[\psi_{\mathrm{reg}}(W;\alpha_0,h_0)]=\mathbb E[g_1(X)-g_0(X)-\alpha]=0,\quad \psi_{\mathrm{reg}}(W;\alpha_0,h_0)=g_1(X)-g_0(X)-\alpha,\qquad (39)

where h=(g1(⋅),g0(⋅))h=\bigl(g_1(\cdot),g_0(\cdot)\bigr) collects the nuisance (i.e., the regression functions).

Treating ∂h\partial_h as a directional derivative, one can quickly check the regression adjustment score (39) is not orthogonal:

∂𝔼[g1(X)−g0(X)−α]∂g1=1≠0,∂𝔼[g1(X)−g0(X)−α]∂g0=−1≠0(40) \frac{\partial\mathbb E[g_1(X)-g_0(X)-\alpha]}{\partial g_1}=1\neq0,\quad \frac{\partial\mathbb E[g_1(X)-g_0(X)-\alpha]}{\partial g_0}=-1\neq0\qquad (40)

Propensity Score Weighted Estimator Another commonly adjustment is via the propensity score, defined as

e(X)=ℙ(D=1∣X),(41) e(X)=\mathbb P(D=1\mid X),\qquad (41)

which is widely used due to its balancing property and being sufficient for unconfoundedness (see Appendix A for more details). The Propensity Score Weighting Estimator is

ATÊprop=1n∑i=1n[DiYiê(xi)−(1−Di)Yi1−ê(xi)],(42) \widehat{\mathrm{ATE}}_{\mathrm{prop}}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\left[\frac{D_iY_i}{\hat e(x_i)}-\frac{(1-D_i)Y_i}{1-\hat e(x_i)}\right],\qquad (42)

where ê(xi)\hat e(x_i) is an estimator of the propensity score, typically a logit model Λ(X′γ)\Lambda(X'\gamma) if estimated parametrically. Similar to the regression adjustment estimator, ATÊprop\widehat{\mathrm{ATE}}_{\mathrm{prop}} will generally not be asymptotically normal unless e(⋅)e(\cdot) correctly specified parametric model (or known).

We can define the moment condition (27) and write down ψprop(W;α0,h0)\psi_{\mathrm{prop}}(W;\alpha_0,h_0) similar to (39), with h=e(X)h=e(X). One can also try taking the partial derivatives ∂h𝔼[ψprop(W;α0,h0)]\partial_h\mathbb E[\psi_{\mathrm{prop}}(W;\alpha_0,h_0)] and verify ψprop\psi_{\mathrm{prop}} does not satisfy the orthogonality condition. For a full proof for this statement using pathwise derivative, we refer readers to Appendix A.

Combining Regression Adjustment and Propensity Score Weighting: doubly robust estimator As foreshadowed above, neither (38) nor (3) is Neyman orthogonal. We next show that combining them together orthogonalize our score and naturally yields the “doubly robust” estimator, which was first coined by Robins and Rotnitzky (1995) and highly recommended in the causal inference literature. We define:

ATÊrobust=1n∑i=1n[Di(Yi−ĝ1(xi))ê(xi)−(1−Di)(Yi−ĝ0(xi))1−ê(xi)+ĝ1(xi)−ĝ0(xi)].(43) \widehat{\mathrm{ATE}}_{\mathrm{robust}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{D_i\bigl(Y_i-\widehat{g}_1(x_i)\bigr)}{\widehat{e}(x_i)}-\frac{(1-D_i)\bigl(Y_i-\widehat{g}_0(x_i)\bigr)}{1-\widehat{e}(x_i)}+\widehat{g}_1(x_i)-\widehat{g}_0(x_i)\right].\qquad (43)

The orthogonalized score is now:

ψdr(Wi,α,h)=Di(Yi−g1(Xi))e(Xi)−(1−Di)(Yi−g0(Xi))1−e(Xi)+g1(Xi)−g0(Xi)−α.(44) \psi_{dr}(W_i,\alpha,h)=\frac{D_i\bigl(Y_i-g_1(X_i)\bigr)}{e(X_i)}-\frac{(1-D_i)\bigl(Y_i-g_0(X_i)\bigr)}{1-e(X_i)}+g_1(X_i)-g_0(X_i)-\alpha.\qquad (44)

Robins and Rotnitzky (1995) showed that (43) will be consistent and n\sqrt{n}-asymptotically normal fairly generally as long as regression functions and propensity score flexibly estimated. One can estimate asymptotic variance as using a plug-in estimator:

V̂r=1n∑i=1n(ψ̂i−ATÊrobust)2.(45) \widehat{V}_r=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\widehat{\psi}_i-\widehat{\mathrm{ATE}}_{\mathrm{robust}}\right)^2.\qquad (45)

The estimator remains valid as long as either the propensity score OR regression model is correctly specified (in parametric context), and hence the name “doubly robust”.

Orthogonality property of Doubly Robust Estimator of ATE The moment condition for ATÊrobust\widehat{\mathrm{ATE}}_{\mathrm{robust}} has an orthogonality property in that

∂h𝔼[ψr(Wi,α,h)]|α0,h0=0,(46) \left.\partial_h\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi_r(W_i,\alpha,h)\bigr]\right|_{\alpha_0,h_0}=0,\qquad (46)

where α0\alpha_0 and h0h_0 denote the population values. See Appendix A for a formal proof of this statement using pathwise derivatives.

Thus, the derivative with respect to g1g_1 vanishes. The same argument shows that perturbing g0g_0 by g0,t(x)=g0,0(x)+th0(x)g_{0,t}(x)=g_{0,0}(x)+th_0(x) yields

ddtM(α0,e0,g0,t,g1,0)|t=0=0. \left.\frac{d}{dt}M(\alpha_0,e_0,g_{0,t},g_{1,0})\right|_{t=0}=0.

Therefore the score ψdr\psi_{dr} satisfies the Neyman orthogonality condition:

∂∂t𝔼[ψdr(W;α0,ηt)]|t=0=0 \left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{dr}(W;\alpha_0,\eta_t)]\right|_{t=0}=0

for every regular perturbation direction of the nuisance parameter η=(e,g0,g1)\eta=(e,g_0,g_1). ■

B Additional Example

Construction of orthogonal score using likelihood in high dimensional regression As an illustration of the construction in Section 4.2.2, consider the following linear predictive model:

Y=Dθ0+X′β0+U,𝔼[U∣X,D]=0,D=X′γ0+V,𝔼[V∣X]=0. \boxed{\begin{aligned}Y&=D\theta_0+X'\beta_0+U,&\mathbb{E}[U\mid X,D]&=0,\\D&=X'\gamma_0+V,&\mathbb{E}[V\mid X]&=0.\end{aligned}}

For simplicity, assume that θ0\theta_0 is scalar, while β0∈ℝdβ\beta_0\in\mathbb{R}^{d_\beta}. The first equation is the main predictive model, and the second equation is introduced only to construct the Neyman orthogonal score.

The pair (θ0,β0)(\theta_0,\beta_0) solves the least-squares problem

maxθ∈Θ,β∈ℬ𝔼[ℓ(W;θ,β)],ℓ(W;θ,β)=−(Y−Dθ−X′β)22, \max_{\theta\in\Theta,\;\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}[\ell(W;\theta,\beta)],\qquad\ell(W;\theta,\beta)=-\frac{\bigl(Y-D\theta-X'\beta\bigr)^2}{2},

where W=(Y,D,X′)′W=(Y,D,X')', θ∈Θ=ℝ\theta\in\Theta=\mathbb{R}, and β∈ℬ=ℝdβ\beta\in\mathcal{B}=\mathbb{R}^{d_\beta}.

The corresponding score functions are

∂θℓ(W;θ,β)=(Y−Dθ−X′β)D,∂βℓ(W;θ,β)=(Y−Dθ−X′β)X. \partial_\theta\ell(W;\theta,\beta)=\bigl(Y-D\theta-X'\beta\bigr)D,\qquad\partial_\beta\ell(W;\theta,\beta)=\bigl(Y-D\theta-X'\beta\bigr)X.

Differentiating once more gives the Hessian blocks:

∂θθℓ(W;θ,β)=−D2,∂θβℓ(W;θ,β)=−DX′,∂βθℓ(W;θ,β)=−XD,∂ββℓ(W;θ,β)=−XX′. \begin{aligned}\partial_{\theta\theta}\ell(W;\theta,\beta)&=-D^2,&\partial_{\theta\beta}\ell(W;\theta,\beta)&=-DX',\\\partial_{\beta\theta}\ell(W;\theta,\beta)&=-XD,&\partial_{\beta\beta}\ell(W;\theta,\beta)&=-XX'.\end{aligned}

Hence, the matrix of expected second derivatives is

J=𝔼[∇(θ,β)2ℓ(W;θ,β)]|(θ,β)=(θ0,β0)=(JθθJθβJβθJββ), J=\left.\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{(\theta,\beta)}^2\ell(W;\theta,\beta)\right]\right|_{(\theta,\beta)=(\theta_0,\beta_0)}=\begin{pmatrix}J_{\theta\theta}&J_{\theta\beta}\\J_{\beta\theta}&J_{\beta\beta}\end{pmatrix},

with

Jθθ=−𝔼[D2],Jθβ=−𝔼[DX′],Jβθ=−𝔼[XD],Jββ=−𝔼[XX′]. J_{\theta\theta}=-\mathbb{E}[D^2],\qquad J_{\theta\beta}=-\mathbb{E}[DX'],\qquad J_{\beta\theta}=-\mathbb{E}[XD],\qquad J_{\beta\beta}=-\mathbb{E}[XX'].

The original score for θ\theta is

φ(W;θ,β)=∂θℓ(W;θ,β)=(Y−Dθ−X′β)D, \varphi(W;\theta,\beta)=\partial_\theta\ell(W;\theta,\beta)=\bigl(Y-D\theta-X'\beta\bigr)D,

but this score is not generally orthogonal to the nuisance parameter β\beta. To enforce orthogonality, define the Neyman orthogonal score

ψ(W;θ,η)=(Y−Dθ−X′β)(D−μX),η=(β′,vec⁡(μ)′)′. \psi(W;\theta,\eta)=\bigl(Y-D\theta-X'\beta\bigr)\bigl(D-\mu X\bigr),\qquad\eta=(\beta',\operatorname{vec}(\mu)')'.

The orthogonalization matrix μ\mu is chosen so that

Jθβ−μJββ=0. J_{\theta\beta}-\mu J_{\beta\beta}=0.

Using the explicit formulas above, this becomes

−𝔼[DX′]−μ(−𝔼[XX′])=0, -\mathbb{E}[DX']-\mu\bigl(-\mathbb{E}[XX']\bigr)=0,

or equivalently,

μ𝔼[XX′]=𝔼[DX′]. \mu\,\mathbb{E}[XX']=\mathbb{E}[DX'].

If 𝔼[XX′]\mathbb{E}[XX'] is invertible, then

μ0=𝔼[DX′](𝔼[XX′])−1=γ0′, \mu_0=\mathbb{E}[DX']\bigl(\mathbb{E}[XX']\bigr)^{-1}=\gamma_0',

where the last equality follows from the linear projection equation D=X′γ0+VD=X'\gamma_0+V with 𝔼[V∣X]=0\mathbb{E}[V\mid X]=0.

Hence, the orthogonal score at the truth is

ψ(W;θ0,η0)=U(D−μ0X), \psi(W;\theta_0,\eta_0)=U\bigl(D-\mu_0X\bigr),

which is orthogonal to first-order perturbations in the nuisance direction.
